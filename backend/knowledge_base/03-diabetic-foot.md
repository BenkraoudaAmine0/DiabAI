# The Diabetic Foot and Ulcers

## Why diabetes affects the feet

Over time, high blood glucose damages nerves (peripheral neuropathy) and
blood vessels (peripheral arterial disease). Neuropathy dulls sensation, so a
blister, cut or foreign object can go unnoticed; poor circulation slows
healing. Together these turn small injuries into ulcers and infections.

## Diabetic foot ulcers

A diabetic foot ulcer is an open sore or wound, most often on the plantar
surface (sole) under the metatarsal heads or on the toes. Ulcers precede most
non-traumatic lower-limb amputations in people with diabetes. Warning signs
on the skin include calluses that develop dark centres, blisters, cracks,
redness, swelling, drainage, and non-healing breaks. Any foot wound that has
not healed within a few days warrants medical attention.

## Warning signs to photograph or show a clinician

- A break in the skin that does not close
- Redness, warmth or swelling around a wound
- Dark or black tissue (possible necrosis)
- Drainage or pus, foul smell
- New pain, or conversely complete loss of sensation
- Changes in foot shape (Charcot foot)

## Daily foot care

Inspect feet daily (use a mirror for the soles), wash with lukewarm water and
dry between the toes, moisturise dry skin but not between toes, never walk
barefoot, trim nails straight across, wear well-fitting shoes and clean,
seam-free socks, and avoid self-treating corns or calluses. Smoking worsens
circulation and should be stopped.

## Medical management

Ulcer care includes offloading pressure (special footwear, casts), removal of
dead tissue (debridement), infection control with dressings and antibiotics
when needed, vascular assessment, and optimising glucose control. Multidisc
iplinary diabetic foot clinics substantially reduce amputation rates. The
American Diabetes Association recommends a comprehensive foot examination at
least once a year for every person with diabetes.
