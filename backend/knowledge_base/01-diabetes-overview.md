# Diabetes Mellitus — Overview

Diabetes mellitus is a chronic metabolic disorder characterised by elevated
blood glucose (hyperglycaemia) resulting from defects in insulin secretion,
insulin action, or both. Chronic hyperglycaemia is associated with long-term
damage to the eyes, kidneys, nerves, heart and blood vessels.

## Type 1 diabetes

Type 1 diabetes results from autoimmune destruction of the insulin-producing
beta cells of the pancreas, leading to absolute insulin deficiency. It usually
onset in childhood or adolescence but can appear at any age. People with type 1
diabetes require lifelong insulin therapy. Symptoms typically begin abruptly
and include excessive thirst (polydipsia), frequent urination (polyuria),
unexplained weight loss, fatigue and blurred vision.

## Type 2 diabetes

Type 2 diabetes is the most common form, accounting for about 90% of cases. It
is characterised by insulin resistance — the body's cells respond poorly to
insulin — combined with a progressive decline in insulin production. Major
risk factors include overweight and obesity (especially abdominal fat),
physical inactivity, family history of diabetes, age over 45, history of
gestational diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidaemia, and belonging to certain
ethnic groups. Type 2 diabetes often develops silently over years, which is
why screening of at-risk individuals matters.

## Gestational diabetes

Gestational diabetes appears during pregnancy and usually resolves after
delivery, but it raises the lifetime risk of type 2 diabetes for both mother
and child. Screening is typically performed between the 24th and 28th week of
pregnancy.

## Common symptoms

Classic symptoms of hyperglycaemia: excessive thirst, frequent urination,
increased hunger, fatigue, blurred vision, slow-healing wounds, recurrent
infections, and tingling or numbness in the hands or feet. Many people with
type 2 diabetes have no obvious symptoms and are diagnosed through routine
screening.

## Why early detection matters

Early detection and good glucose control reduce the risk of complications
such as diabetic retinopathy (eye damage), nephropathy (kidney disease),
neuropathy (nerve damage), foot ulcers, and cardiovascular disease (heart
attack, stroke). Regular monitoring of HbA1c, blood pressure, cholesterol,
and annual eye and foot examinations are recommended for every person living
with diabetes.
